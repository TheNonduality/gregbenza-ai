## The failure

The drone is a Flywoo Explorer LR 4 (V1), 4S, with a DJI O4 Air Unit Pro video
system. It was four days old and flying normally.

/>

Then a gust caught it on landing and put it in a tree. I replaced all four
props, went back up, and the video feed degraded mid-flight — picture breaking
up, then gone. I landed it and stopped flying.

I have no oscilloscope, no bench and no electronics background. The available
diagnostic tool was a phone camera.

## So I photographed everything and asked

The method is one loop, run repeatedly: **photograph the hardware → show the AI
→ get one hypothesis → test it → photograph the result.**

The photos do the work, and how they are taken decides whether it works at all.
Board out of the airframe. Part labels sharp enough to read. A wide shot for
context on where the part sits. And every time the AI names a component,
re-shoot that component, to confirm both sides are looking at the same object.

That first round produced results. Before the crash, a photo of the camera
mount identified which hardware revision had shipped: a solid 3D-printed cradle
with no damping balls means V1. After the crash, the teardown photos produced
the fault — one of the air unit's two ceramic antennas was gone, its U.FL coax
connector torn off the circuit board with the solder pad still attached.

## Repair or replace

Fixing a ripped pad properly means scraping back the solder mask, exposing the
trace underneath, and soldering a new U.FL connector onto it. That is specialist
micro-soldering. A search for a local shop that would take it on came up empty.

There were two paths: specialist repair or replacement. The AI laid out both,
assessed the pad repair as beyond the tools on hand, and did not propose a third
option. I bought a replacement air unit and kept the broken one as a practice
target.

## Replacing the broken part didn't fix it

New air unit in, camera moved across, and the drone was still faulty: jerky
video, stuck in low-power mode, and then a crash. The visible break had been
real, and fixing it changed nothing.

It took about five teardowns to find the actual fault. The 3-in-1 harness
carrying signal between the video transmitter and the flight controller was
damaged. I swapped the harness and flew the same day, and it worked.

Two days later the signal was still weaker than it should have been. The cause
was a pinhole tear in the antenna coax with the shielding showing through —
almost invisible, and the third separate fault in one airframe.

## What the photos couldn't do

Photos did not find the harness damage, and they did not find the coax tear.

Photos plus AI narrowed every search and named the parts, which is most of the
work when you don't know the correct terms for the components. But the two
faults that kept the drone grounded were both found by teardown and inspection.

The generalisable point is not specific to drones: obvious damage can hide
further damage. After replacing the broken part, re-test everything before
concluding the repair is finished. Here, two faults were declared fixed before
the airframe was actually serviceable.
