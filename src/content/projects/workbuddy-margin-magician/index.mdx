## The workflow that was me

A sales team runs on the same questions every week. Who's selling what.
Which shops went quiet. What the month looks like with a week left.

In July 2025 I started answering them a new way: export a line-item
spreadsheet from the ERP, upload it into a chat, and ask in plain
language. The first one is logged on July 16 — a sales report and a
one-line request to review the month's rep numbers. It worked. So I did
it again. Hundreds of conversations over the next ten months, on ChatGPT
first and Claude later, and somewhere in there the workflow earned two
names.

## WorkBuddy

Eight days after that first upload, in a voice conversation on a drive, I
talked through why the thing I was doing felt bigger than a spreadsheet
trick. The transcript survived, and it reads like a spec for software
that wouldn't exist for another year: an assistant you sit down with and
talk through your day, that asks questions back, brings up the customers
you should be thinking about, and turns the conversation into a call
list.

That was the WorkBuddy idea — not a report generator, a **work
companion**. The same conversation already contained the problem that
would eventually cap it: chat memory was too small to hold a working
week, and a custom GPT forgets everything between sessions. I knew what I
wanted, and I knew the chat window couldn't hold it. I built it there
anyway, because that's where I could build.

## Margin Magician

The other name belonged to the numbers. The ERP's line-item export is not
a thing you can hand to an AI raw — it's full of traps. Orders that were
cancelled but still present. Sample product that looks like revenue.
Internal transfers dressed up as sales. Three different dates, only one of
which means anything.

Margin Magician was a custom GPT whose entire job was to read that export
correctly: a set of golden rules — ship date is the only date that
matters, dead statuses don't exist, samples never count, internal zones
aren't sales — plus a column-by-column reading of the file. It ran for
months as the team's answer machine.

## The Halloween crash

On October 31, 2025, it stopped working. Same instructions, same kind of
file — but the model underneath the custom GPT had changed, and rules
that had run reliably for three months began failing. The diagnosis
session went nowhere, because there was nowhere for it to go: the rules
lived in a prompt, on a platform, under a model, and I controlled none of
the three. That became the defining lesson of the era — and it's why the
app that came later keeps its arithmetic in plain code, where a model
update can't touch it.

## The move, and the morning of charts

On April 2, 2026 the whole operation moved to Claude: a project named
WorkBuddy, and — uploaded the same day — a document called the Margin
Magician parsing protocol. That upgrade mattered more than it looks. The
rules stopped being a prompt that lived inside one platform's product and
became a **written document** that could travel anywhere.

The Claude era was short and dense. The first day alone produced customer
list extractions and full rep-performance breakdowns. By late May I could
type one request and get a morning's worth of revenue charts and
reconciliations — my project folder still holds a dozen of them stamped
with the same date, May 26.

Two days later, May 28, two new files appear in that same folder:
`cloudflare-worker.js` and an HTML file with the app's name on it.

## What survived

The chat era ended there, but almost nothing was thrown away. The golden
rules written for Margin Magician in April are, nearly word for word, the
sales laws running in [the production app](/projects/one-worker-backend/)
today — ship date only, dead statuses don't exist, samples never count.
And WorkBuddy's wishlist — surface the customers, build the day's call
list, ask questions back — is a fair description of what the app's home
screen now does every morning.

Ten months of chat was the prototype. It just didn't know it yet.
