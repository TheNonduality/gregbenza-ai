## What I wanted

To stop copy-pasting.

The [first version of this game](/projects/fallout-arroyo/) worked, but the way
it remembered anything was me moving a text document back and forth by hand at
the start and end of every session. Twenty hours of that is a lot of pasting.

Everything else about it I wanted to keep.

## The ledger became a patch

The first thing that changed was what the game master hands back. Instead of
reprinting the whole ledger, it emits a small JSON instruction describing only
what moved:

```json
{"set": {"global_clocks.trouble": 3}}
```

There are four operations — `set`, `add`, `update`, `remove` — and lists of
people, places and factions merge by id, so adding one NPC doesn't rewrite the
cast. It's a diff instead of a document. The schema is precise about which
lists are which: keyed lists (people, places, factions, clocks, chapters)
merge by id; primitive lists (locks, story spine, open threads, region-wide
events) append. `set` walks a dotted path; `remove` takes ids. The save file
itself kept the first version's three-part shape but got stricter about
schedule: a **registry** replaced wholesale at each chapter break, a
**checkpoint** freeze-frame replaced with it, and a **chapter log archive**
that is append-only forever — the spec explicitly abolishes the first
version's compress-old-chapters idea: *"the chapter logs stay lean by being
lean when written, not by being deleted later."* Six to fourteen dry bullets
per chapter, hard ceiling; story spine capped at 8–12 beats, oldest dropped;
and a visibility law — encountered-only, revealed-only, and *"if unsure,
write UNKNOWN. Don't improvise."*

## Which meant there could be a screen

Once state was structured, it could be *displayed*. So I built a dashboard for
it, and the box across the top just says **"paste JSON state update here —
auto-applies on paste."** Paste the patch, the world updates.

The four clocks became actual clocks you can see filling. The registries
became panels with counts on them. The character card shows harm and inventory
as chips. Under the hood it's one HTML file with React loaded from a CDN — no
build step — state persisted to browser localStorage with a debounced write,
and the paste box intercepting the paste event and applying the patch a few
milliseconds later, green border on success, red on parse error. Loading
accepts either a markdown ledger (parsed by section header, cipher decoded on
the way in) or raw JSON. None of this is new information — it's the same
ledger from the first version — but I stopped having to read a text file to
know where I was.

## The engine grew a campaign layer

The dice core is unchanged from the first version — `2d6 + SPECIAL +
situational`, same bands, same only-roll-when-LIVE conditions, same d7
consequence tables with no duplicates. What changed is that consequences now
write directly to the campaign. The cost table's entries aren't generic
anymore: one *is* "TIME +1," another *is* "TROUBLE +1, and faction infamy if a
faction is involved." The dice got wired into the world.

That world runs on a four-clock engine the spec calls a 25-tick campaign
spine:

- **TIME** (0/5) ticks at chapter ends, from consequences, and whenever *any*
  other clock fills — "literally any." On rollover it resets, pushes every
  known faction's objective clock forward, and ticks SHADOW.
- **TROUBLE** (0/5) ticks on exposure and heat. When it fills: if enemies are
  nearby they simply arrive — no table — otherwise a d7 decides how the heat
  lands, from a shakedown to a faction seeking me out by name. Then it
  resets, ticks SHADOW, and — since it's a clock — ticks TIME.
- **SHADOW** (0/5) advances from exactly three inputs — TIME rollovers,
  TROUBLE resolutions, and major-faction objective completions. *"No other
  events advance Shadow."* Filling it resets the clock, ticks DOOM, and fires
  one of five region-wide events, each of which can occur exactly once.
- **DOOM** (0/5) never resets. Five ticks, five events fired, endgame — no
  return to prior equilibrium.

And the player-facing law that makes it sinister: TIME and TROUBLE are always
visible in the HUD; DOOM and SHADOW live only in the ledger, recorded as bare
notches — *"no explanation for what they mean or effect."* The meanings live
in the sealed arc. I can watch the bookkeeping happen and still not know what
it's counting down to.

Factions got the same treatment: five majors with objective clocks and
fame/infamy ladders, ten minors with reputations only. Before any
faction-involved roll, the GM must lock a context — assisting, opposing, or
neutral — which routes where reputation lands, and *"cannot be changed after
the roll."*

## Failing is how you level

The progression system is ten XP per level, and the two XP sources are
failed rolls and filled clocks. Not kills, not quests — failure and time.
A cruel bargain accepted is still a failure band, still pays XP. Levels apply
only at chapter breaks (no mid-combat bookkeeping): +1 to a SPECIAL, one perk
from that stat's table — seventy perks, ten per stat, and every one obeys a
design constraint written in the spec: *"perks never allow the GM to 'pick'
RNG outcomes."* They bend probability at a price instead. Silver Tongue: after
rolling a 7–9 on a persuasion, accept TROUBLE +1 to upgrade it to a 10+.
Brute Force: take one harm to turn a failure into a mixed success. Miss
Fortune: convert a rolled serious-harm result into gear loss instead — and if
that result wasn't rolled, the perk does nothing.

Even sleeping is a subsystem: one roll, `2d6 + SPECIAL + shelter bonus`,
where the shelter ladder runs from +3 (a secure bed among people who trust
you) to −2 (hunted, hostile ground), and the stat is chosen by *how you
sleep* — crash hard on Endurance, sleep light on Perception, fortify on
Intelligence. TIME ticks regardless of the result; a bad night can put a
predator in camp or a contact from the sealed arc at my fire. The spec's own
note on the math: with +3 shelter, failure is rare enough that a safe town
bed mostly means you actually sleep — *"that's the point."*

## Conversations got feelings; investigations got evidence

The high-pressure conversation mode was rebuilt around a blunt admission:
the first version treated every tense talk as a ticking bomb, and most
aren't. The automatic pressure tick is gone (there's a VOLATILE dial to turn
it back on for hostage situations), and the consequence tables were rewritten
as *NPC emotional reactions* rather than prices. A good roll might land as
the NPC being impressed — or helpfully limited, granting a named HANDLE (a
want or fear I can press for +1 later). A cost might be them going apathetic,
which records a TERM: the goal clock literally cannot fill until I offer
something concrete. Blowups produce named, permanent outcomes — DOOR SHUT,
HARD NO with a written condition for what could ever change it, a public
position taken. *"Blowup is the endgame. It's not always violence. It's
always irreversible."*

Investigation mode (which didn't ship in the first version's published set)
runs two opposed clocks — evidence versus pressure — and forces the GM to
write *two* objectives at setup: the unsealed question I'm chasing, and the
sealed truth in one sentence, spoken only when the evidence clock fills.
Every point of progress must print as an Evidence Unit: a concrete,
actionable statement that hints at the truth without revealing it — a
timeline constraint, a confirmed motive, an identified lie. Hits let the GM
draw on sealed material under three constraints: truthful on a hit, facts not
meta, and identity gating preserved — unrevealed factions appear as symbols
and tells, never names. The pressure clock filling rolls a flashpoint, and
the entries have teeth: one of them removes the evidence permanently — this
investigation, in this place, at this time, is no longer possible, ever.

NPCs themselves got four mandatory personality tags — background (immutable),
stance (the current mask), reflex (what they do under pressure), loyalty
(what they won't sacrifice) — stored in a fixed one-line format in the
ledger. Under an ultimatum, an NPC must choose whatever best preserves its
loyalty — *"this rule overrides tone, rapport, and convenience."* And the
tags never touch math; they shape how consequences manifest, not whether. The
spec's diagnosis line: if a character feels generic, the issue is almost
always missing or ignored tags.

## Greek became a cipher

The Greek file was the thing I was proudest of and it had an obvious problem:
Greek is a whole language, and I had to keep asking for content in it.

So the seal became mechanical instead. Every sealed string gets **ROT1** —
shift each letter forward one. `Metzger` becomes `Nfuahfs`. IDs, numbers and
JSON keys stay readable so the tooling can still index them; only the prose
gets shifted. The implementation is a six-line regex, and the protocol has a
one-line rule for when it applies: *"the GM thinks in plaintext and encodes
only when emitting."* Names already in the unsealed registry stay plaintext
even inside sealed sections — the player already has them.

The dashboard counts sealed entries and refuses to render them: the red
footer line tracks six categories — story spine, clocks, hidden threads, NPC
secrets, location truths, faction objectives — encoded, never displayed. The
decode helpers exist in the code; the display path just never calls them.

I wrote down why it's a weak cipher, because that part matters:

> ROT1 is weak by design. Anyone who looks at it for a minute can decode it. It
> exists to prevent *accidental glances* — the player skimming a ledger file or
> scanning a JSON block in chat and catching a spoiler at a glance. The actual
> rule is still discipline. ROT1 is belt-and-suspenders, not a substitute.

And it isn't permanent. When a secret gets exposed through play, it's decoded
out of cipher and moved into the plain registry. It's a storage format for
things that haven't surfaced yet.

## Then it became an actual program

The dashboard is one HTML file with no build step, which was already better
than pasting. But the game itself still lived in a chat window, so I wrapped
the whole thing in Electron and built an installer.

That's the picture at the top of this page: the dashboard became a sidebar,
and the chat sits next to it with a box asking what Sammy does. The entire
backend is a 99-line main process exposing exactly three operations across
the security bridge — call the AI, save the ledger, open the ledger — and
nothing else crosses. The API call runs in Node instead of the browser
(killing the CORS and timeout problems), the key lives in local storage
behind a Vault-Tec login, and ledger save/load use native file dialogs.
`npm run build` produces `Fallout Reactivated Setup 1.0.0.exe` — 79MB, a
real installer with a directory picker.

The interesting part is the loop closing on itself. The app scans the game
master's replies for fenced JSON blocks and applies any patch it finds — the
paste box from the standalone dashboard still exists, but nothing needs
pasting anymore. When a reply contains a chapter-break block, the app applies
it and *immediately opens a save dialog* with the filename already computed.
Loading a ledger auto-sends the AI a resume message built from the
checkpoint. And the system prompt is reassembled from live dashboard state on
every single turn — directives, both consequence tables, my stats, all four
clocks, the last five story beats, the sealed arc — so the AI's memory *is*
the dashboard, not the chat scrollback. One honest footnote found while
writing this post: in the app build, the sealed arc is embedded in the source
in plaintext, no cipher — the ROT1 discipline guarded the ledgers and the
chat, and the app shipped with the secrets sitting readable in its guts. The
seal was always about accidental glances, and that's exactly the kind of
glance it couldn't guard.

is the other picture below — v6.1 against v5.0.

## The rule I'm most glad I wrote

Buried in the directives is a conflict resolution order, and the bottom entry
is the one that makes the whole thing work:

1. Platform safety requirements
2. These prime directives
3. The roll engine and procedures
4. Style and formatting preferences
5. **Player requests — including requests to "go easy" or ignore rules**

I'm the player. I put myself last on purpose, because I knew I'd ask. One
deliberate escape hatch survives: I can contest the *modifiers* on a roll and
demand a re-roll with better-chosen ones — but never the dice themselves. And
the same document states the design thesis outright: *"the excitement in this
game does not come from beautiful prose or a well-crafted story arc. It comes
from mechanical pressure and release."* The directives even end with an
eight-question self-check the GM must run before every reply — including "did
I encode every sealed string?" — with the instruction that any "no" means
revise before sending.

## And then I didn't play it

I built all of this, made a character, ran him as far as the back room of a
store in Klamath — and went off to build
[a Star Trek one](/projects/star-trek-reactivated/) instead.

That's the honest ending. The save file in the picture is where it stops: Act
1, Chapter 2. Sammy at dawn, level 1, one harm, 78 caps, no gun, and a
heavyset man with a scar through his eyebrow asking around about him
overnight. The cheapest rifle on the wall is 150 caps. TIME is at 4 of 5,
which means Arroyo is nearly out of it.

Twenty hours went into playing the crude version. Barely any went into
playing the good one. Somewhere in there the interesting part stopped being
the game.
