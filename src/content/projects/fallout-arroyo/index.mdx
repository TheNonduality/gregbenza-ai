## What I wanted

A Fallout 2 campaign I could actually play, with an AI as the game master. Not
a chatbot doing improv — a referee that rolled dice, tracked my injuries, and
told me no.

I'd never built anything like this. No game design background, no software
background.

## The part I didn't see coming

I thought the hard part would be the rules. Rules are imaginable: dice, stats,
what happens when you fail. I wrote those first and they were fine.

The problem was that the AI forgot the entire world every time I closed the
chat. A whole evening of story, every faction I'd annoyed, the guy I'd killed —
gone. I didn't know the words for what I was missing. I just knew the game kept
starting over.

## So the whole thing became files

The one thing I understood was that you could attach documents to a project and
the AI would read them. That's it. That was my entire toolkit, so that's what
the game got built out of — one numbered document per job:

- **`02` the rules** — how dice work, what a failure costs
- **`03` the format** — what every reply has to look like
- **`05` the opening** — how a new character gets made
- **`06` the secrets** — everything I wasn't allowed to know
- **`07` the ledger** — where I actually was

No database. No code. Five text files and a lot of pasting.

## The Greek one

Writing the secrets file is where it got stupid in a way I liked.

I was building the game master *and* playing the game. So every twist I wrote
down, I'd already read. The villain, the endgame, the clock that decides when
the world tips — I'd spoiled all of it by typing it.

So I had that one file written in Greek. I don't read Greek. The AI does. I put
a line at the top telling it never to reveal, translate, or quote the file to
me, and used it only for motivation and offscreen movement.

It worked better than it should have. And it turned out to be load-bearing, not
a gimmick — the four global clocks that drive the whole campaign are *only*
defined in that file. You can't run this game properly without handing the AI a
document you can't read.

## Copy-pasting the ledger

This is the unglamorous half, and it's most of the actual work.

At the end of a session I'd type `SCENE BREAK` and the AI would print an updated
ledger — where I was, who was with me, my injuries, every clock, every faction's
opinion of me, a one-line summary of each chapter so far. I'd paste that back
into the document. Next session, I'd paste the document back at the AI.

That was the memory. Me, moving a text file back and forth by hand, every single
time. Not elegant. But the world stopped resetting.

The ledger picked up the same trick as the Greek file, which I hadn't planned:
it split itself in two. `STORY SPINE | UNSEALED` for what my character knew,
`STORY SPINE | SEALED | GM ONLY` for what was actually true, plus hidden threads
and an offscreen-truth section. Same document, two audiences.

## How I stopped it cheating

The rules exist mostly to take away the AI's discretion.

A roll is `2d6 + SPECIAL + situational`, banded 2–6 fail, 7–9 succeed-but-pay,
10+ clean. When something goes wrong, the game master isn't allowed to *pick*
what goes wrong — it rolls a d7 on a consequence table. No duplicates. It can
reskin a result to fit the scene but it can't throw one out because it doesn't
like it. Then it has to print the dice, the modifiers, the total, and the table
result before it narrates a word.

That last rule did more than anything else. A referee that has to show its
arithmetic can't quietly decide the story went a nicer way.

Combat, travel, and tense conversations each got their own subsystem with their
own countdown clocks, added when I hit the thing a single roll couldn't handle.
A conversation has two opposed clocks — what you're trying to get, and how close
the room is to blowing up — and an explicit rule that no single roll, however
good, ends it early.

## It worked — about 20 hours of it

Twenty hours of real play. Act II, Chapter 14 by the time I stopped.

Sammy, the Chosen One of Arroyo: level 5, four perks, two companions. Ambushed
and killed the slaver boss Metzger at a collapsed overpass. Broke a prisoner out
of the Den. Rode a caravan into New Reno, ran an errand for the NCR, and bought
a road north from a crime family by agreeing to three jobs I shouldn't have
agreed to. Doom clock at 2 of 5. One body not yet connected to me.

The files above are the real ones. Four specs plus my ledger should drop your AI
into the campaign exactly where I left it — outside a bathhouse in New Reno, in
some trouble.
