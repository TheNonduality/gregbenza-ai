## What I wanted

A Fallout 2 campaign I could actually play, with an AI as the game master. Not
a chatbot doing improv — a referee that rolled dice, tracked my injuries, and
told me no.

I'd never built anything like this. No game design background, no software
background.

## The part I didn't see coming

I thought the hard part would be the rules. Rules are imaginable: dice, stats,
what happens when you fail. I wrote those first and they were fine.

The problem was that the AI forgot the entire world every time I closed the
chat. A whole evening of story, every faction I'd annoyed, the guy I'd killed —
gone. I didn't know the words for what I was missing. I just knew the game kept
starting over.

## So the whole thing became files

The one thing I understood was that you could attach documents to a project and
the AI would read them. That's it. That was my entire toolkit, so that's what
the game got built out of — one numbered document per job:

- **`02` the rules** — how dice work, what a failure costs
- **`03` the format** — what every reply has to look like
- **`05` the opening** — how a new character gets made
- **`06` the secrets** — everything I wasn't allowed to know
- **`07` the ledger** — where I actually was

No database. No code. Five text files and a lot of pasting. Character creation
is the whole of `05` — fourteen lines. There's no character sheet: the tutorial
NPCs ask you questions ("what are you good at?") and your answers become your
SPECIAL modifiers.

## How I stopped it cheating

The rules exist mostly to take away the AI's discretion, and the discretion is
removed piece by piece:

**The roll.** `2d6 + SPECIAL_MOD + SITUATIONAL_MOD`, banded 2–6 fail, 7–9
succeed-but-pay, 10+ clean. SPECIAL stats are modifiers (−1 to +3), and your
*approach* picks the stat — force is STR, noticing is PER, lying is CHA,
"maybe this works" plans are LCK — locked before the dice, no re-picking after.

**When to roll at all.** A roll only happens if the moment is LIVE, and LIVE
is defined: risk, cost, opposition, uncertainty, pressure, or irreversibility —
any one of the six. None of them? No roll; the thing just happens. And no free
rerolls, ever: retrying the same action requires materially changing the
situation, otherwise the world moves instead.

**The situational modifier is computed, not vibed.** Leverage scores 0–2,
friction scores 0–2, and the modifier is `clamp(leverage − friction, −2, +2)`.
Leverage has to be *earned* — create an advantage before the roll, commit to a
narrow intent that closes off safer options, accept a downside up front, or
exploit established fiction. The spec even ships a "Not Leverage" list:
attacking normally isn't leverage, using a weapon as intended isn't leverage,
describing the same advantage twice isn't leverage.

**Consequences are counted, then rolled.** The band decides *how many*
consequences (a bare 7 costs you twice; a 2–3 means two separate fallout
rolls), and a d7 table decides *which* — harm, time, resources, exposure,
gear, hostility, a cruel bargain. No duplicates. The GM may reskin a result to
fit the scene ("Resources" becomes "burn goodwill") but can't discard one it
doesn't like. Then it must print the dice, the modifiers, the total, the band,
and the table rolls before narrating a word — a ten-field roll card, every
time. A referee that has to show its arithmetic can't quietly decide the story
went a nicer way.

**Even the wounds have math.** Harm is a 0–5 track (2–3 wounded, +1 friction;
4 crippled, +2; 5 dying). Armor is a 0–3 tier and `net harm = max(0, base harm
− armor)`. Serious hits roll an injury table — and one entry, Bleeding, starts
its own four-segment clock that ticks every combat exchange until treated.

## Clocks instead of hit points

Combat, travel, and tense conversations each got their own subsystem when I
hit the thing a single roll couldn't handle — and they're all the same shape:
countdown clocks that tick on defined triggers.

- **Combat** runs on a duration clock (4, 6, or 8 segments by scale) that
  ticks *automatically after every exchange* — an exchange being ten to thirty
  seconds of chaos, not one bullet. When it fills, the fight resolves off an
  endgame table. Rolling a 10+ earns a combat edge the GM must stop and offer:
  one of the options is spending it to *not* tick the clock.
- **Travel** ticks a progress clock per leg and a danger clock alongside it;
  the danger clock filling rolls a travel incident, which becomes the next
  scene.
- **Conversation** — the piece I'm proudest of — runs two opposed clocks: the
  goal (what I'm trying to get) and the pressure (how close the room is to
  blowing up). An explicit rule says no single roll, however good, resolves
  the conversation — only a filled clock does. A 10+ lets me choose: +2
  progress, or +1 progress and −1 pressure. And the consequence tables are
  keyed to the stat I used — body approaches pull from one table group, mind
  from another, social from a third — so the GM can't shop for the table it
  finds dramatic. If the pressure clock is three-quarters full when I win
  anyway, an optional rule rolls a cost regardless: the spec calls it a
  poisoned victory.

## The format is also rules

Document `03` treats the AI's *output* as a system with the same rigor as the
dice. Every gameplay message opens with a four-line HUD — campaign, chapter,
clock time, location ID, state emoji, my stats and harm, a one-sentence
sit-rep, and any visible clocks. Messages come in two densities, FRAME (scene
opens, camera moves, a roll resolved) and PULSE (fast back-and-forth), with
rules for which is legal when. Unknown values must be written `UNKNOWN`, not
guessed.

Two pieces of furniture do the anti-drift work. `🔒 LOCKS` pins one to three
facts that cannot change unless a roll changes them — added specifically
because early play kept "re-opening" doors the fiction had shut. And a
`⚙️ CHANGE LOG` must list every irreversible change *even in short messages* —
the spec's words: no sneaking major changes inside dialogue.

## The Greek one

Writing the secrets file is where it got stupid in a way I liked.

I was building the game master *and* playing the game. So every twist I wrote
down, I'd already read. The villain, the endgame, the clock that decides when
the world tips — I'd spoiled all of it by typing it.

So I had that one file written in Greek. I don't read Greek. The AI does. The
header tells it never to reveal, translate, or quote the file, and to use it
only for four things: motivation, offscreen movement, clocks, and causality.

It worked better than it should have. And it turned out to be load-bearing,
not a gimmick — the file holds the campaign's fixed truths, the four global
clocks and what each tick of them *means*, a five-rung doom ladder where every
rung is a described world-state, a table of five region-wide events that each
fire exactly once, and fifteen factions — five major, each with its own
objective clock whose every tick is a scripted omen, plus fame and infamy
ladders with named consequences. You can't run this game properly without
handing the AI a document you can't read. Even the ledger's sealed sections
carry their instructions in Greek — the note telling the GM to write there in
Greek is itself in Greek.

## Copy-pasting the ledger

This is the unglamorous half, and it's most of the actual work.

At the end of a session I'd type `SCENE BREAK` and the AI would print an
updated ledger — a HEAD holding current state (campaign meta, the four global
clocks, my stats and conditions, story spine, active clocks, and registries of
every person, place and faction *encountered so far* — the visibility law says
encountered-only, revealed-only), followed by an append-only scene block:
what happened, the rolls that mattered, and then eleven delta sections written
as explicit transitions:

```
P:PC01 moved: L:WLD03 → L:NR01
CLK:SHADOW: 4/5 → 5/5 → 0/5 (completion/reset)
Regionwide event roll: 1d5 → E5
```

Everything has a typed ID — `P:` people, `L:` places, `F:` factions, `CLK:`
clocks — so a fact survives being rewritten. I'd paste that block back into
the document, and paste the whole document back at the AI next session. That
was the memory. Me, moving a text file back and forth by hand, every single
time. Not elegant. But the world stopped resetting.

The ledger picked up the same trick as the Greek file, which I hadn't planned:
it split itself in two. `STORY SPINE | UNSEALED` for what my character knew,
`STORY SPINE | SEALED | GM ONLY` for what was actually true, plus hidden
threads and an offscreen-truth section — sealed content written in Greek, in
the middle of my own save file. The only legal path from sealed to unsealed is
the REVEAL LOG: when play exposes a truth, it gets logged in plaintext, and
the shipped ledger has a real one — the region-wide migration event becoming
visible in play.

## It worked — about 20 hours of it

Twenty hours of real play. Act II, Chapter 14 by the time I stopped.

Sammy, the Chosen One of Arroyo: level 5, four perks, two companions. Ambushed
and killed the slaver boss Metzger at a collapsed overpass. Broke a prisoner
out of the Den. Rode a caravan into New Reno, ran an errand for the NCR, and
bought a road north from a crime family by agreeing to three jobs I shouldn't
have agreed to. Doom clock at 2 of 5. One body not yet connected to me.

The files above are the real ones. Four specs plus my ledger should drop your
AI into the campaign exactly where I left it — outside a bathhouse in New
Reno, in some trouble. Two honest caveats on the files: the export from the
original docs mangled the emoji (restored by inference), and the conversation
protocol cuts off partway into its final section — the anti-drift emergency
commands — which survives only in [the second version](/projects/fallout-reactivated/).
