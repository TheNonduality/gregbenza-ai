## What I wanted

To fly the drone I'd owned for four days.

  type="video"

Then a gust caught it on landing and put it in a tree. I replaced all four
props, went back up, and the video feed bled out mid-flight — picture breaking
up, then gone. I landed it and stopped flying.

I don't have an oscilloscope or a bench or any real electronics background. What
I had was a phone.

## So I photographed everything and asked

The whole method is one loop, run over and over: **photograph the hardware →
show the AI → get one hypothesis → test it → photograph the result.**

The photos do the work, and how you take them decides whether it works at all.
Board out of the airframe. Part labels sharp enough to read. A wide shot so
there's context for where the thing sits. And every time the AI names a
component, re-shoot that component, so you're both certain you're looking at the
same object and not two different ones.

That first round worked. Before the crash it had already told me which hardware
revision I'd been shipped — from a photo of the camera mount, because a solid
printed cradle with no damping balls means V1. After the crash it read the
teardown photos and named the fault: the video antenna's tiny coax connector had
been torn off the circuit board, taking its solder pad with it.

## The fork I didn't want

Fixing a ripped pad properly means scraping back the solder mask, exposing the
trace underneath, and soldering a new connector onto it. That's specialist
micro-soldering. I looked for a shop that would do it and found nothing local.

What I wanted was a third option, and there wasn't one. The AI didn't invent one
either — that's the part I remember. It laid out the two real paths, said the
repair was beyond my bench, and let me choose. I bought a replacement air unit
and kept the broken one as something to practise on with nothing at stake.

## Replacing the broken part didn't fix it

This is the part worth the post.

New air unit in, camera moved across, and the drone was *still* wrong. Jerky
video, stuck in low-power mode, and then it crashed. The obvious break had been
real, and fixing it changed nothing.

It took about five teardowns to find the actual fault: the 3-in-1 harness
carrying signal between the video transmitter and the flight controller was
damaged. Swapped the harness, flew the same day, and it worked.

Two days later the signal was still weaker than it should have been. That turned
out to be a pinhole tear in the antenna coax with the shielding showing through
— almost invisible, and the third separate fault in one airframe.

## What the photos couldn't do

Find the harness. Or the coax tear.

Photos plus AI narrowed every search and named the parts, which is genuinely
most of the work when you don't know what things are called. But the two faults
that actually kept the drone grounded were both found by hands, taking the thing
apart again, and looking.

The transferable bit isn't about drones: **obvious damage hides other damage.**
After you replace the broken part, re-test everything before you decide you're
done. I decided I was done twice and was wrong both times.
